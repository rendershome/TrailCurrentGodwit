# No single box powers Godwit safely

No off-the-shelf product found combines a protected 12 V vehicle input, an ignition input, stored-energy hold-up and a clean 5 V / 5 A output for a Raspberry Pi 5. None of the Pi-specific products found documents ISO 7637-2 / ISO 16750-2 load-dump or cold-crank survival. The closest single products each miss one piece. The **CarPiHAT PRO 5** has ignition-latched shutdown and "up to 5A" to a Pi 5 but no hold-up. The **Geekworm UPS Scap 5V5A** and **X1205** have hold-up and 5-6 A but no ignition input. The **Mini-Box DCDC-USB-200** has the best ignition logic on the market (6-34 V input, programmable off-delay and hard-off, 11.2 V battery cut-off, a 5 V or 12 V output setting, spread spectrum) but no energy storage. The most practical purchase today is a DCDC-USB-200 in Automotive mode, wired to the Pi's power button. Set to 5 V it feeds a Pi 5 directly. Set to 12 V it feeds a 7-36 V CM5 base such as the Waveshare CM5-DUAL-ETH-4G/5G-BASE. Add a small supercap stage only if crank dips and sudden battery disconnects must be covered. For a broadcast engineer the larger problem is that **no Pi power product publishes a switching frequency, ripple or EMI figure**. The only fixed-frequency supplies with published figures (Mean Well RSD-30 at 110 kHz, Pololu D36V50F5 at about 500 kHz) put harmonics straight into the 520-1710 kHz AM band. That missing data, together with the missing transient data, is why Godwit should publish its own open-hardware front end: surge stopper, ideal-diode reverse protection, a 2 MHz-class spread-spectrum buck, an LTC3350-class supercap stage and an ignition microcontroller. ADI and TI reference designs exist for each block.

## What Godwit has to power, and why 5 A is tight

Godwit's Pi 5 image sets `usb_max_current_enable=1`, which tells the Pi to assume a 5 A supply and lift its USB port current limit. Waveshare gives the same instruction for any Pi 5 fed from a source that does not negotiate USB-PD: set `usb_max_current_enable=1` or `PSU_MAX_CURRENT=5000` ([Waveshare UPS HAT (E) docs](https://docs.waveshare.com/UPS-HAT-E), vendor documentation). This matters for the choice of supply. **Boards that feed the 40-pin header or pogo pins (CarPiHAT PRO 5, Witty Pi 5, Geekworm X120x) never negotiate USB-PD, so the image's existing setting is required, not optional.** The Geekworm UPS Scap 5V5A is the exception. It feeds the Pi's USB-C port with a PD handshake through a supplied C-to-C adapter ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2026/10/05/geekworm-ups-scap-5v5a-a-supercapacitor-ups-hat-for-raspberry-pi-5/), news).

The load is close to 5 A. The only sourced figure in the device list is Nooelec's datasheet draw of **270-330 mA per NESDR SMArt v5** ([Nooelec](http://www.nooelec.com/datasheets/100700), vendor datasheet), or about 0.54-0.66 A for two dongles. The rest of the budget is the researchers' unmeasured estimate. It puts the Pi 5 SoC and RAM under load at about 2-2.5 A and the 10.1" DSI panel at about 0.9 A. Adding two ESP32-S3 dials, USB audio and the CAN FD HAT brings the total to about 5 A at 5 V, roughly 25 W. **Treat this as an inference to measure on the bench, not a datasheet total.** It has two consequences. A supply rated at exactly 5 A has no margin. And any hold-up store sized for "a Pi" (2-3 A) is too small. The Waveshare 2-CH CAN FD HAT already on the Pi takes an 8-28 V input (8-26 V in another place in the same wiki) behind a 2 A fuse, with no 5 V output rating. It cannot be counted on as the Pi's supply ([Waveshare wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/2-CH_CAN_FD_HAT), vendor wiki, internally inconsistent).

The CM5 path changes the problem. Waveshare's CM5 bases and HMIs take **7-36 V** directly, and the CM5-DUAL-ETH-4G/5G-BASE also has the 40-pin header the CAN HAT needs ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/cm5-dual-eth-4g-5g-base.htm), vendor page). On a CM5 the front end must provide a protected, **stabilized 12 V** with ignition logic. It no longer has to supply 5 V at 5 A, because the base's own regulator does that.

**The CarPiHAT PRO 5 is the only current Pi product designed for a car.** It provides 5 A to a Pi 5 through a new switching IC and better inductors, draws under 1 mA when off, and implements the shutdown sequence a head unit wants. The Pi latches its own power on a GPIO, a service watches the switched-12 V ignition input, and the latch releases when the CPU halts. The reference script waits 10 s after key-off ([Core Electronics](https://core-electronics.com.au/carpihat-pro-5-car-interface-dac.html), reseller page; [CarPiHat wiki](https://github.com/gecko242/CarPiHat/wiki/Quick-Start-Guide), search extract). It has **no supercap or battery**. It relies on permanent battery power plus the ignition wire, so it covers key-off but not loss of the battery feed or deep crank sag ([RPi forum](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=361432), forum). For Godwit it also overlaps the existing stack. It brings its own CAN port, DAC and RTC, and the researchers infer that stacking it with the 2-CH CAN FD HAT would need a check for SPI chip-select and GPIO conflicts. Its input range, buck IC, CAN controller and the licence of the PRO 5 schematics were not confirmed, so it does not yet meet Godwit's open-hardware requirement.

**The supercap and Li-ion HATs solve the opposite problem.** The Geekworm UPS Scap 5V5A gives about **9 s at 5 V / 5 A** from 100 F, or about 3.5 min at 0.5 A, recharges in about 15 min, and signals power loss on GPIO26 with INA219 telemetry ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2026/10/05/geekworm-ups-scap-5v5a-a-supercapacitor-ups-hat-for-raspberry-pi-5/), news). Nine seconds at full load is enough only if the panel and USB loads are shed at once on power-fail. It is not enough to ride a full Linux shutdown under load, which is the researchers' inference. Its 5 A is also shared with its USB-A port. The X1205 is the only board with stated headroom above 5 A, at **6 A max from 9-18 V** ([Geekworm wiki](https://wiki.geekworm.com/X1205), search extract). But an 18 V maximum input leaves no margin for a 12 V system's transients, and Li-ion cells in a dashboard raise temperature questions the notes do not answer. The **Witty Pi 5** is the most open of the HATs: RP2350 firmware and software are on GitHub, it takes 6-30 V, delivers 5 A and can trigger on VIN thresholds ([UUGear](https://www.uugear.com/product/witty-pi-5/), vendor page). It has neither an ignition input nor hold-up. The researchers suggest adding ignition sense with an opto-isolator to a spare GPIO, or reading ignition state from CAN, which Godwit already has.

Several other boards fall short of 5 A. The Sequent Super Watchdog delivers "only 2.5A @5V", and its Multichemistry sibling gives 2.5 A continuous / 3 A peak from 10-24 V ([The Pi Hut](https://thepihut.com/products/super-watchdog-for-raspberry-pi), search extract). Others are gone. The Mausberry car supply is discontinued and never listed the Pi 4/5. PiJuice is retired, the Sixfab UPS HAT is end-of-life, and Juice4halt targets the Pi B+/2/3 ([Mausberry](https://www.mausberrycircuits.com/collections/car-power-supply-switches/products/3a-car-supply-switch), search extract; [Sixfab](https://community.sixfab.com/t/ups-hat-discontinued/2281), vendor forum). Seeed's LTC3350 supercap module exists, but it is built for the reComputer R1000 and DigiKey quotes a 33-week lead time ([DigiKey AU](https://www.digikey.com.au/en/products/detail/seeed-technology-co-ltd/110992004/24814462), distributor).

## The DCDC-USB-200 is the closest off-the-shelf answer

Among stand-alone boxes, only the Mini-Box family packages real car-PC power logic. The **DCDC-USB-200** takes **6-34 V**, is fused and TVS-protected, draws under 1 mA in standby in Automotive mode, and its output can be set to 5, 6, 9, 12, 13.5, 18 or 24 V by jumper, or 5-24 V in 0.25 V steps over USB. It is rated to 160 W and sells for **USD 89**, "usually ships in 24h" ([Mini-Box](https://www.mini-box.com/DCDC-USB-200), vendor page). Its manual adds the ignition behaviour. Eight jumper presets set OFFDELAY (ignition-off to shutdown pulse) and HARDOFF (pulse to power cut), from 5 s/60 s to 2 h/never. During HARDOFF the unit cuts power if the battery falls below **11.2 V**. It shuts down at 34 V, clamps at 34-36 V and limits input current to 15 A, and "Emissions are reduced using 2 out of sync, spread spectrum buck-boost converters" ([DCDC-USB-200 manual](https://www.cartft.com/support_db/support_files/DCDC-USB-200-manual.pdf), manufacturer manual hosted by a reseller). The manual contradicts itself on two points. Its introduction gives a 6-24 V output range while its jumper table includes 5 V, and it lists both -40 to +85 °C and -20 to 65 °C operating ranges. It also publishes **no 5 V current table, no switching frequency, no CISPR 25 class and no ISO 7637-2 result**. Linux tools are at github.com/mini-box/dcdc-usb, with no licence stated on the product page.

Wiring it to Godwit is an integration job, not a plug-in feature. The researchers infer the following. The shutdown pulse, designed for an ATX power-switch header, would drive the Pi 5's PWR_BUT pads or a GPIO through an opto-coupler. Whether its pulse levels suit the Pi 5 directly was not confirmed. Because the box runs from permanent battery power and stays on through the OFFDELAY, **the vehicle battery itself provides the "hold-up" for a key-off shutdown**. Stored energy is then needed only for crank dips below 6 V and for sudden battery disconnects, so a small supercap stage is enough and a battery UPS is not needed. For a CM5 base, set the box to 12 V and feed the 7-36 V input. That is the most documented route.

The other stand-alone options each cover one need. The **OpenUPS2** is a real battery UPS (3x LiFePO4 18650, shutdown pulse on low battery, 1 µA deep sleep, USD 119), but it outputs only 12-24 V at 5 A, takes an 11-24 V input and states no ignition input ([Mini-Box OpenUPS2](https://www.mini-box.com/OpenUPS2), vendor page). It fits the CM5 path better than the Pi 5 path. The **Mean Well RSD-30G-5** is the best-documented clean 5 V rail: 9-36 V input, **5 V / 6 A at 70 mVp-p**, -40 to +55 °C without derating, EN 50155 railway approval and EN 55032 emissions (Class A conducted, Class B radiated) ([Mean Well RSD-30 spec](https://www.meanwell.com/Upload/PDF/RSD-30/RSD-30-SPEC.PDF), vendor datasheet). Railway approval is not the same as ISO 7637-2, and the unit has no ignition logic. The **Pololu D36V50F5** (5.5-50 V input, reverse protection to 40 V, power-good output, and an EN pin precise enough for a resistor-divider battery cut-off) is a good bench and prototype regulator, but it makes no automotive transient or EMI claim ([Pololu](https://www.pololu.com/product/4091), vendor page). Dashcam hardwire kits are rated at about 3 A (marketplace listings, low reliability), and BlackVue's Power Magic Pro is discontinued ([BlackboxMyCar](https://www.blackboxmycar.com/pages/everything-you-need-to-know-about-power-magic-pro), reseller).

RVs and trucks on 24 V need extra care. The DCDC-USB-200's 34 V shutdown leaves almost no margin for a 24 V system's load dump. The researchers infer that a 24 V install needs an upstream surge stopper or a 9-36 V / 18-75 V railway brick instead. For reference, ADI's DC2062A surge-stopper demo limits up to **100 V load dumps to about 25 V** at the output ([DC2062A schematic](https://www.analog.com/media/en/technical-documentation/eval-board-schematic/DC2062A2-SCH.PDF), vendor documentation). That is the class of protection none of the Pi products above claims.

## AM and FM: published switching frequencies point into the broadcast bands

This section keeps sourced data and engineering inference separate, because the sourced data is thin.

**Sourced data.** Only four switching-frequency figures exist in the research. Two are for products and two are for reference designs. The Mean Well RSD-30's block diagram gives **fosc 110 kHz** ([Mean Well RSD-30 spec](https://www.meanwell.com/Upload/PDF/RSD-30/RSD-30-SPEC.PDF), vendor datasheet). Pololu gives the D36V50F5 as **about 500 kHz at heavy load**, with a light-load power-save mode that stays above 20 kHz ([Pololu](https://www.pololu.com/product/4091), vendor page). TI's TIDA-01524 uses a **400 kHz** spread-spectrum synchronous buck ([TI TIDA-01524](https://www.ti.com/tool/TIDA-01524), vendor reference design). TI's TIDA-01492 uses a **2.2 MHz** synchronous buck optimized for CISPR 25 Class 5 conducted emissions ([TI TIDA-01492](https://ti.com/tool/TIDA-01492), vendor reference design). The DCDC-USB-200 claims two out-of-sync spread-spectrum converters but gives no frequency. **CarPiHAT PRO 5, Geekworm X120x and Scap, Witty Pi 5, Daygreen and Tobsun publish no switching frequency, ripple (except Mean Well's 70 mVp-p) or EMI data at all.** No in-vehicle AM or FM interference test of any of these units was found.

**Inference: where the harmonics land in 520-1710 kHz.** The table is arithmetic on the sourced fundamentals. It is not a measurement.

| Converter | Fundamental (source) | Harmonics inside 520-1710 kHz | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean Well RSD-30G-5 | 110 kHz (datasheet) | 5th-15th: 550, 660, 770 ... 1650 kHz, every 110 kHz | Fixed, no spread spectrum stated: a comb of eleven carriers across the band |
| Pololu D36V50F5 | about 500 kHz (vendor page) | 2nd and 3rd: about 1000 and 1500 kHz | Fixed at heavy load; drifts in power-save mode at light load, so the spurs move |
| TIDA-01524-class buck | 400 kHz (reference design) | 2nd-4th: 800, 1200, 1600 kHz | Spread spectrum smears each into a band |
| TIDA-01492-class buck | 2.2 MHz (reference design) | None; fundamental above 1710 kHz | Only sub-harmonic, pulse-skipping or beat products could fall in band |
| DCDC-USB-200 | Unpublished | Unknown | Spread spectrum claimed; two unsynchronized converters can produce beat products |

The researchers draw three conclusions, all inference that needs confirming with a near-field probe and the actual receivers. First, a fixed sub-MHz converter near an AM front end produces discrete heterodyne whistles or carriers at predictable channels. The 110 kHz comb is the worst case because it hits about every eleventh 10 kHz channel across the band. Second, a converter switching at **2 MHz or above** keeps its fundamental out of the AM band entirely, which is easier to manage than a 400-500 kHz part whose 2nd-4th harmonics fall inside it. Third, spread spectrum is a trade-off for AM listening. It lowers the peak of each harmonic, which is what CISPR 25 quasi-peak and peak limits reward. But it spreads that energy over a wider bandwidth, which can raise the noise floor heard in a 9-10 kHz AM channel instead of leaving a few clear channels with one spur each. For DX-quality reception, a fixed 2 MHz+ converter with good filtering, whose few spurs can be mapped and avoided, can beat a spread-spectrum part at 400 kHz. Light-load behaviour matters too. A converter with a power-save mode, as the Pololu has, can change frequency as the panel's backlight current changes, which moves its spurs around. Whether the DCDC-USB-200 does this is not published.

**Inference: FM (88-108 MHz).** No source gives FM-band emissions for any product. FM interference from a buck converter usually comes from switching-edge ringing and the hot loop, not from the switching frequency. A harmonic number in the hundreds carries little energy, but fast edges with tens-of-MHz ringing can radiate through the input wires and the cable to the Pi. Shielded inductors, an input π-filter, a ferrite or common-mode choke on the output lead, and short input loops matter more here than the choice of fundamental. In the Godwit enclosure, the supply competes with the Pi 5, the DSI ribbon, USB 3.0 and the RTL-SDRs' own clocks as an FM noise source, so FM noise should be measured on the full system, not the supply alone. The earlier research recommended a separately fed, powered hub for the SDRs with the dongles on short extensions away from the panel and compute board, as general practice rather than a sourced finding. A dedicated filtered 5 V branch for that hub belongs in the front-end design.

## What an open-hardware Godwit front end needs

Each block of the front end has a published reference design. No single design covers 12/24 V input to 5 V / 5 A with both ISO 7637-2 and CISPR 25 data, so they have to be combined. The researchers propose this chain. Each component class comes with a sourced example where one exists.

**Connector and fusing.** Three inputs: permanent battery, ignition (switched 12 V) and ground, with an input fuse. This matches the constant-plus-switched pattern the CarPiHAT and DCDC-USB-200 both use.

**Reverse-battery protection.** An ideal-diode controller with back-to-back FETs. TIDA-01492's smart-diode stage drops about **20 mV** ([TI TIDA-01492](https://ti.com/tool/TIDA-01492)), far less than a Schottky at 2 A input current.

**Load dump and ISO 7637-2.** For 24 V systems, and for 12 V where a TVS alone is not enough, use a surge stopper. The **LT4363** (4-80 V input, adjustable clamp, latch-off -1 or auto-retry -2 versions) has a 12 V ISO 7637-2 demo, DC2062A, that limits up to 100 V dumps to about 25 V at 3 A ([ADI LT4363](https://www.analog.com/en/products/lt4363.html), vendor). ADI's AN-2083 pairs an LT4356-1 surge stopper with an LT8609A buck for a 12/24 V to 5 V / 3 A front end ([ADI AN-2083](https://wcm-cce.cldnet.analog.com/media/en/technical-documentation/app-notes/an-2083.pdf); details only from a search summary because the fetch timed out). TI's **TIDA-01179** is a 30 W, 12 V front end tested to ISO 7637-2:2004 pulses 1, 2a and 3a/b at level IV ([TI TIDA-01179](https://www.ti.com/tool/TIDA-01179), vendor). At 30 W it is the closest power class to Godwit's estimated 25 W.

**Hold-up.** An **LTC3350**-class supercap controller on the intermediate bus. It charges, balances and monitors 1-4 series supercaps for 12 V ride-through, and its DC1937B demo is labeled "12V NOMINAL 25W" ([ADI DC1937B](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/evaluation-hardware-and-software/evaluation-boards-kits/dc1937b.html), vendor). That rating matches Godwit's estimated load almost exactly. Vishay's EDLCBU-12V reference design (LTC3350 plus 6 parallel strings of 4 EDLC cells) publishes downloadable design files but no hold-up figure ([Vishay](https://www.vishay.com/en/landingpage/reference-designs/EDLCBU-12V/), vendor). The researchers sized the store themselves (unsourced). Thirty seconds at 25 W is 750 J. A 4-series stack discharged from 10.8 V to 5 V gives about 45.8 J per farad of stack, so it needs about **16 F of stack (about 65 F per cell)**. A 10 s brown-out ride-through needs about a third of that, and the 2-3 s for an orderly `poweroff` after a sudden disconnect needs only a few farads. For comparison, Geekworm's 100 F at 5 V gives about 9 s at 25 W, which is consistent with this arithmetic.

**Main regulator.** A **2 MHz-class synchronous buck with spread spectrum that can be turned off**, rated about 6 A at 5 V so there is margin over the estimated 5 A, with an input π-filter and a shielded inductor. TIDA-01492 (5 A, 2.2 MHz, CISPR 25 Class 5 conducted) is the closest reference ([TI](https://ti.com/tool/TIDA-01492)). TIDA-01524 shows the cold-crank and start-stop side: 3.5-36 V operation with CISPR 25 Class 5 conducted results ([TI](https://www.ti.com/tool/TIDA-01524)). For the CM5 path, the same stage becomes a stabilized 12 V output, which needs a buck-boost so it holds 12 V through cranking. Being able to switch spread spectrum off is the researchers' recommendation for AM DX use. It lets the builder choose between a lower peak for compliance and fixed, avoidable spurs for listening.

**Ignition and battery logic.** A small microcontroller that debounces ignition and runs a programmable OFFDELAY and HARDOFF (copying the DCDC-USB-200's model). It also applies a battery cut-off, sends a pulse to the Pi's PWR_BUT pads or a GPIO, and reports telemetry over I2C to a Linux daemon. Industry cut-off levels give the defaults: Mini-Box's hard cut-off is **11.2 V** ([DCDC-USB-200 manual](https://www.cartft.com/support_db/support_files/DCDC-USB-200-manual.pdf)), and BlackVue advises **no lower than 12.0 V, or 12.2-12.5 V in cold climates** ([BlackVue forum](https://forum.blackvue.com/hc/en-us/articles/49160733840921-Battery-Protection-Features-Low-Voltage-Cutoff), vendor support forum). The board should also hold the Pi's power latch after halt to get CarPiHAT-style off-draw under 1 mA. The researchers suggest an ESP32-class part, since Godwit already builds ESP32-S3 firmware for its dials. An RP2350 as on the Witty Pi 5, whose firmware is already open, is another option.

Prior open designs are hobby-grade but useful starting points: Compizfox's **RPiCarPowerHAT** (LM2596 buck, ignition MOSFET with Pi self-latch, low-voltage cut-off), zbchristian's Raspi_PowerSwitch, Benoit3's RaspPowerHat, and Scott Baker's 5-cell supercap UPS with shunt balancing ([RPiCarPowerHAT](https://github.com/Compizfox/RPiCarPowerHAT); [sbelectronics/ups](https://github.com/sbelectronics/ups)). The LM2596 is a 150 kHz part by general knowledge, not from the notes, so its harmonics would land in the AM band. That is one more reason to start the regulator stage from the TI or ADI references instead.

## Conclusion

The market splits a vehicle head unit's power problem in two. Car-PC vendors (Mini-Box) solved ignition logic for x86 boards a decade ago but publish no 5 V current table or EMI data. Pi accessory vendors solved hold-up for desk and kiosk use and assume a clean wall adapter. **Nobody sells the combination for a 25 W Pi 5 or CM5, and nobody publishes the two numbers a radio-first head unit cares about most: switching frequency and conducted emissions.** That gap is Godwit's opportunity. An open front end with a published fundamental, a spread-spectrum on/off option, CISPR 25-style conducted plots and ISO 7637-2 pulse results would be the first Pi power board a broadcast engineer could evaluate before installing it.

The short-term decision depends on the compute platform. For a Pi 5 today, a DCDC-USB-200 set to 5 V with its pulse wired to PWR_BUT gives proven ignition sequencing for USD 89. Measure its unpublished noise on the AM band before trusting it near the receivers. Moving to a CM5 on a 7-36 V base makes the open front end simpler: it then needs a protected, held-up 12 V rail and a small ignition controller rather than a 6 A low-noise 5 V regulator. That is a strong reason to make the CM5 path Godwit's reference vehicle build. The first open measurements to make are 5 V droop at 5 A and the AM and FM noise floor of the candidate supplies.
